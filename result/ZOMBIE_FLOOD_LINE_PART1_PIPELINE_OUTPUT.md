## 0. Quyết định đã chốt (setup)

- **Thời lượng gốc người dùng muốn:** 30 phút. Vì script chỉ viết đủ 1800 từ (~12 phút theo công thức `số giây × 2.5 từ`), đã quyết định **chia thành nhiều Part** — **Part 1 = 12 phút = 90 chapter** (8 giây/chapter), dựa trên script hiện có. Các Part sau (2, 3…) sẽ viết thêm script mới khi làm tiếp.
- **Visual beats:** AI tự thiết kế (không phải người dùng cho beat).
- **Narration:** bake sẵn vào từng clip (không lồng tiếng hậu kỳ).
- **Phong cách ảnh/video:** theo đúng "LOCKED STYLE DNA" trong docx — hand-drawn 2D cartoon, nét mực đen sketchy, graphic-novel storybook; bảng màu slate blue/charcoal/gray/wet brown/rust + điểm nhấn amber/mustard/moss green/sparing red; lighting cinematic low-key, damp haze, puddle reflections; world texture gạch vỡ, cửa thép rỉ, dây leo, ống nước nhỏ giọt, rào chắn; tỉ lệ 16:9; không bao giờ: người thật/3D/Pixar/anime/photorealism.
- **Filter-safe vocabulary** (dùng xuyên suốt mọi prompt sau này): bite→lunge/grab · blood→dark stains/grime · kill, dead→stop, down, still · gun, shoot→flare, flashlight beam · weapon→tool (pipe, crowbar, plank) · corpse, body→fallen shape · explosion→concussive burst of light · scream→shout/gasp · infected→turned · hammer, nail→mallet, peg · zombie→shambler (chỉ dùng nếu bị từ chối).
- **Ràng buộc chuẩn của người dùng (áp dụng mọi dự án):** mọi prompt tạo ảnh/video giao cho người dùng phải **tự đầy đủ (self-contained)**, không placeholder/"như trên"/"same as before" — họ chỉ copy-dán trực tiếp. Nếu cần ghép nhiều đoạn (ví dụ 90 clip 8-giây ghép thành 1 video 12 phút) phải **nói rõ, không ngầm hiểu**.

## 1. STATE 1 — Story + Documentary Script (đã duyệt)

**Title:** THE FLOOD LINE

**Synopsis:** When a ruptured water main turns an underground transit shelter into a rising tomb, two strangers — Cole, a quiet mechanic known for keeping his head down, and Ivy, a former lab tech who never stopped running the numbers — must cross six flooding tunnel sections to reach the last sealed stairwell to the surface, racing both the tide and the shambling tide of turned pulled upward by the current, before a betrayal that propped a bulkhead open floods their escape route with more than water.

**Full voiceover script (1800 words, confirmed as final for Part 1 — không kéo dài thêm):**

Six hours before the city lost its underground, nobody believed the water could rise faster than the trains could run.

It started as a maintenance note nobody read: a hairline crack in a century-old water main, three stories below street level, directly above the last operating transit shelter in the district. By the time anyone noticed the drip had become a stream, the stream had become a current, and the current had already found its way into the tunnels where two hundred people had been told they were safe.

Cole had been one of the first to volunteer for shelter maintenance. He knew every rusted valve and dead junction box in the lower concourse, the kind of knowledge nobody thanked him for until the lights started flickering on their own. Ivy had arrived three days earlier with a duffel bag and a notebook full of numbers nobody asked her to keep — infection curves, pressure readings, the math of how long a sealed space can hold against what's pressing on the other side of its doors. Neither of them had planned on needing each other. The flood changed that in under a minute.

The first alarm wasn't the sirens. It was the sound of the lower platform doors buckling inward under a weight that shouldn't have been pushing from that side — not water pressure alone, but bodies, dozens of them, carried up from the flooded access tunnels by a current they couldn't fight and didn't need to. The turned don't drown like the living do. They just keep moving, however the water decides to move them.

Cole got Ivy out of the processing line seconds before the platform lights went under. They didn't speak. There wasn't time to agree on anything except which direction was still dry.

What the shelter's residents didn't know — what even the engineers hadn't flagged — was that the lower concourse had been built on a slope. Every drop of water that found its way in wasn't pooling. It was sliding, chapter by chapter, toward the one stairwell still sealed against the surface. The same current that was drowning the lower levels was also herding everything alive and everything that used to be alive toward that single exit point. There was only ever going to be one way out, and it was about to become very crowded.

They moved east first, through the old ticketing hall, because east was dry and dry felt like safety. It wasn't. The hall's far doors had been barricaded from the inside weeks earlier, stacked with filing cabinets and a vending machine nobody had the heart to admit was permanent furniture now. Cole put his shoulder into the gap anyway, over and over, while Ivy counted the seconds between the groans coming from the flooded stairwell behind them — not human groans, not exactly — and did the math on how many more of those sounds her nerves could survive.

The barricade gave. What came through the gap with them wasn't air. It was the smell of standing water and rust, and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the first sign that the hall on the other side wasn't empty.

Three shapes, moving the way only the turned move — the shoulders first, the feet dragging to catch up, no hurry in a body that no longer gets tired. Cole and Ivy didn't run from them. Running on wet tile is how people fall, and falling is how people don't get back up. They walked fast, low, along the one dry ledge still above the waterline, and let the shapes stumble into the water they were trying to avoid.

By the time they reached the maintenance corridor — the one place in the entire shelter where Cole had the only working key — the water behind them had risen past the first stair. They had maybe forty minutes before it reached the second. Ivy didn't say the number out loud. She wrote it on her forearm in marker instead, the way she'd started writing every number that mattered, in case her voice gave out before her hands did.

The maintenance corridor was supposed to be the easy stretch. It was the one part of the shelter Cole had mapped with his own hands, every junction box, every drainage grate, every spot where the concrete had already started to crumble long before any of this began. What he hadn't mapped — because nobody had told him — was the auxiliary door at the far end, the one connecting to the old rail yard. It had been locked since before the outbreak. It was not locked now.

They found it standing open roughly a hand's width, propped by a folded length of pipe wedged under the frame. Someone had done that on purpose. Someone had wanted that door open badly enough to risk what might come through it.

That someone was Harlan, the shelter's groundskeeper, the quiet older man who'd kept the generators running since the first week and never asked for recognition for it. Cole had trusted him with half the shelter's spare parts. What Cole hadn't known — what nobody had known — was that Harlan's daughter and her two children had been trapped on the far side of the rail yard for six days, and that an hour earlier, with the water already rising, he had made a choice he'd spend the rest of whatever life he had left trying to live with. He'd propped the door to let his family run through toward the maintenance corridor, toward safety, toward the one exit that still led up. He hadn't known the water had already turned that exit into the only path for everything else trying to follow the current out of the flooded yard.

Cole and Ivy didn't learn any of that from Harlan. They learned it from what came through the gap behind his family — eleven shapes, maybe more, pouring out of the rail yard's flooded dark in a slow, relentless column, following the same rising water everyone else was trying to escape.

Harlan's daughter made it through the gap. Harlan didn't get the chance to close it behind her.

From that point, there was no path that wasn't the hard one. Cole wedged what he could against the auxiliary door — a service cart, a folded ladder, anything with weight — buying distance, not safety. Ivy took Harlan's daughter and the two children ahead, through the last dry stretch of the maintenance corridor, toward the stairwell that the entire shelter's remaining water was now sliding toward from every direction at once.

The stairwell itself was the shelter's last sealed exit, a heavy bulkhead door rated to hold back floodwater for exactly the kind of event nobody believed would happen on this side of the river. It had one flaw nobody had accounted for: it opened inward. Every gallon of water pressing against it from the flooding concourse made it harder to pull, not easier, and the water was still rising behind them, carrying its slow column of shapes closer with every passing minute.

Cole reached the bulkhead first and understood the problem immediately. Pulling it open against that pressure wasn't a two-person job. It was closer to four, and there were two adults and two children, one of whom was too small to do anything but hold on.

Ivy did the math out loud this time, because some numbers need saying. If they waited for the water to equalize on both sides, the door would open easier — and so would every other door between here and the flooded concourse, for everything still shuffling toward the only dry sound left in the building: the sound of people trying not to drown.

They didn't have a better plan. They had thirty-one minutes, by Ivy's marker-stained arm, and a door that wanted four hands more than they had.

Cole braced his shoulder against the bulkhead's edge, found the leverage point he'd used a hundred times on stuck valves and frozen hinges, and pulled until something in the old hinge groaned loose. Ivy threw her full weight into the gap the second it opened, wedging her whole body as a stop before the door could swing back. Harlan's daughter pushed the children through first. The door held a hand's width longer than it needed to.

Behind them, the water reached the final landing exactly as the last of them cleared the threshold — and with it came the column, closer now, close enough that the sound of dragging feet on wet concrete was no longer background noise. It was the loudest thing in the stairwell.

They climbed. Four flights, in water that rose with them step for step, never quite catching the backs of their heels, never quite far enough behind to stop being terrifying. Somewhere on the second flight, the lights failed completely, and the only light left came from Cole's flashlight beam, swinging wild across wet concrete walls, catching, for one unbearable second, the shape of something climbing the same stairs two landings below.

They didn't look back again.

The surface door was rusted shut in a way no amount of shoulder could fix, until Ivy found the fire axe clipped to the inside wall — a detail nobody had mentioned in six weeks of shelter orientation, because nobody had expected to need it from this side. Three strikes opened a gap. The fourth opened the door.

Cold air. Real air, not the recycled, damp breath of the shelter. Gray daylight, rain-streaked and thin, but daylight. For one full second, nobody in that stairwell remembered there was still a city above them, or what might be waiting in it.

They climbed out into a street they didn't recognize anymore — flooded gutters running brown with runoff, abandoned cars nosed into each other at odd angles, a barricade of furniture and shopping carts collapsed across the intersection like it had given up holding anything back days ago. Somewhere beyond the haze, a flare went up, slow and orange against the gray sky, answered a moment later by another, farther off, like two distant watchtowers checking that the other was still standing.

Harlan's daughter asked, without words, just a look, what came next. Nobody had an answer yet. Behind them, through the half-open stairwell door, the sound of water still rising against rusted steel kept going, patient, unhurried, the way everything down there moved now.

Cole looked back once. Ivy didn't. She was already counting again — the number of flares, the distance between them, how long until whatever answered the second one found its way to this street instead.

They had made it out of the flood line. Nobody told them yet what was waiting on dry ground.
